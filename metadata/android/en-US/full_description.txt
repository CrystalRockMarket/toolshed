<p><b>The shed where the self-hosted tools live.</b></p><p>Toolshed is an offline reference for running services on your own hardware. Every one of the 51 entries answers the same five questions: what is it for, how hard is it to install, what does it cost in memory and disk, what does a working compose file look like, and what will go wrong.</p><p>It is the thing you open on the sofa with a laptop on your knees while a container refuses to start, rather than the thing you open after you have already lost the afternoon.</p><h2>What is in it</h2><ul><li><b>51 services</b> across photos and media, files and documents, productivity, chat and collaboration, automation, monitoring, identity and security, networking, databases, business, and home</li><li><b>Installation guides</b> written as ordered steps, not bullet-point feature lists, including the order things need to happen in</li><li><b>A working compose file for every service</b>, with the values that cause first-run trouble left as environment variables and a note on how to generate each one</li><li><b>Memory, disk, and core estimates</b> per service, so you can tell before you start whether it will fit on the box you have</li><li><b>Notes on what goes wrong</b> and why, which is the part most documentation leaves out: the read-only mount that prevents a category of data loss, the Postgres image you must not replace, the port that makes video calls silently carry no audio</li><li><b>5 starter stacks</b>: complete multi-service setups for a small VPS, a photo vault, a media box, remote access, and family paperwork</li><li><b>A resource planner</b> that totals a shortlist and tells you whether it fits a Raspberry Pi, a mini PC, a NAS, or a small VPS</li></ul><h2>Offline, and empty of permissions</h2><p>Every guide, compose file, and stack ships inside the app. There is nothing to fetch and nothing that can change under you.</p><ul><li><b>No permissions of its own.</b> The manifest declares none: no internet access, no storage, no files, no location, no identifiers. Nothing can be granted because nothing is requested, and Android enforces that rather than the author promising it</li><li><b>No network requests, no analytics, no crash reporting, no account</b></li><li><b>The only two things it can do</b> are open a documentation link in your browser and put text on your clipboard</li><li><b>Your marks stay on your device.</b> Shortlist and running lists are stored locally and are three preferences, not a database</li></ul><h2>Honest about the numbers</h2><p>The resource figures are planning estimates drawn from real installations, not vendor guarantees. They are deliberately conservative and describe typical steady-state use rather than peak. Use them to rule options out and compare against each other, not to size a machine to the byte. The app says so on the About screen too.</p><h2>Licences are not glossed over</h2><p>Some popular services are not open source. n8n, Outline, Invoice Ninja, NocoDB, HashiCorp Vault, and Redis all use source-available licences rather than free software ones. They are included because they are genuinely good at what they do, and every one of those entries says plainly which licence it is and what it means for you. Toolshed does not bundle any of them; it documents them as services you run yourself. Toolshed itself is GPL-3.0-or-later.</p><h2>Who made this</h2><p>Brought to you by Kituwa IT. If you would rather someone else kept your infrastructure running, that is what we do. Toolshed stays free and self-hosted either way, and it is licensed GPL-3.0-or-later, so you are free to inspect it, change it, and run your own copy.</p>
